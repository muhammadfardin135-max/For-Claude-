# Josh Braun — Poke the Bear / Zone of Resistance

## 1. Core thesis
Prospects have an automatic resistance reflex to anything that pattern-matches
to "a salesperson is selling me." Persuasion fails because it triggers that
reflex; the job is to lower resistance, not to push harder. The mechanism is a
neutral question that makes the prospect notice a problem themselves — "the
best way to persuade is to let other people persuade themselves." Cold outreach
aims to start a conversation, never to pitch.

## 2. Operating frameworks

### The 4T cold email template
- **Use when:** Cold email, B2B, first touch.
- **Steps:**
  1. **Truth** — state one observable, verifiable fact about this person or
     this type of person that traces back to how you can help. "What is true
     about this person that you can observe?" (approaching deadline, an event,
     employee turnover, a hiring pattern).
  2. **Think** — a sentence that makes them question their current method. Ask
     how they are "balancing / dealing with / knowing for certain" that their
     current approach is best.
  3. **Third-party validation** — show how someone else got the outcome,
     phrased with "without" or "no": "hit your quota *without* working 60 hours
     a week."
  4. **Talk** — a low-pressure ask: "might you be open to talking about this?"
     / "No rush, but if you're interested…"
- **Why it works (per source):** Truth earns the right to be read, Think opens
  the gap, validation proves it's solvable, and a low-pressure ask avoids
  re-triggering resistance at the last line.
- **Failure mode:** Source gives none explicitly; implied failure is a "Truth"
  that is generic (not observable) which collapses the email into a template.

### Poke the Bear
- **Use when:** Cold call or cold email where the prospect does not perceive a
  problem yet. Josh Braun's stated position: "95% of salespeople don't need to
  overcome objections, they need to poke the bear."
- **Steps:**
  1. Identify a big, expensive problem the prospect may not know they have.
  2. Ask a **neutral** (not leading) question that illuminates it.
  3. Say nothing. Let them do the reasoning.
- **Why it works (per source):** A leading question triggers resistance; a
  neutral question creates "a moment of reflection" and the prospect talks
  themselves into the next step.
- **Failure mode:** Making the question leading turns it back into a pitch.

### Lowering the Zone of Resistance
- **Use when:** Any first contact.
- **Steps:** Strip out language that salespeople always use. Substitute
  neutral, specific, observable wording.
- **Why it works (per source):** Overused sales phrases are the trigger — "'Save
  money' automatically puts prospects in the Zone of Resistance because that's
  what salespeople always say."
- **Failure mode:** Source gives none in the material read.

## 3. Verbatim language patterns

| Phrase / pattern | Where it goes | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| "I often hear that for orgs your size, it takes a few days to calculate and send statements to reps. How does that compare with your experience?" | cold call / email — poke the bear | Names a specific expensive problem, then hands them the judgement. Neutral, not leading. |
| "How are you lowering the risk of losing your best employees next month?" | 4T — Think sentence | Question form that opens a gap without asserting they have a problem |
| "Acme is using us to retain top talent without promises of cushy perks and pay." | 4T — third-party validation | Proof phrased as "without X", where X is the painful default |
| "According to Gartner, 72% of companies rely on that crutch." | 4T — validation support | Third-party stat positions their current method as the crutch |
| "No rush, but is this worth a conversation in the next quarter?" | 4T — close | Low-pressure ask; "no rush" and a distant timeframe remove pressure |
| "might you be open to talking about this?" | close | Permission-based ask |
| "I noticed on your website that [X] is a customer. Congrats on getting traction with such an established brand, which is undeniably no small feat for an early stage startup." | first sentence — show your homework | Proves observation, not automation |
| "Looks like you have 12 SDRs making cold calls to HR. Built a process that's getting SDRs 15-17 live conversations with HR Directors per 50 dials compared to 1-2 before." | first sentence — contrast | Current-state vs. possible-state, in their own numbers |
| "Unlike being an amateur wine connoisseur, reps can't smell the butter aroma of a good inbound lead." | first sentence — hobby/interest | Pattern: "Unlike [their hobby], [their role] can't [analogous thing]". Ports to any hobby. |

## 5. Non-negotiables and prohibitions
- **DO NOT** pitch in a cold email — "the goal of sending cold emails is to
  start a conversation, not to make a sales pitch."
- **DO NOT** use "save money" or similar stock benefit language — it puts the
  prospect straight into the Zone of Resistance because it is what every
  salesperson says.
- **DO NOT** use leading questions when poking the bear — they must be neutral
  or they read as a pitch.
- **DO NOT** open with "Hope you're doing well."
- **DO NOT** use "I know you're super busy, so I'll keep this brief."
- **DO NOT** use "Just bumping this to the top of your inbox."
- **DO NOT** use "following up," "checking in," or "touching base" in follow-ups
  — he has a whole piece on 8 follow-ups that avoid these words.

## 6. Diagnostic questions
- "How does that compare with your experience?" — after naming a problem
  others have. Tests whether the pain is live for them, without asserting it.
- "How are you [lowering the risk of / dealing with / knowing for certain] X?"
  — tests whether they have a deliberate method or a default.
